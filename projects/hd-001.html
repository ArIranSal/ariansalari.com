HD-001

Autonomous solar-charged perimeter surveillance drone

An autonomous quadcopter that patrols a residential perimeter and recharges itself between cycles — and a study in letting the energy budget dictate the architecture. Onboard solar math ruled out self-powered flight, so the harvest is split: cells on the airframe for top-up and loiter margin, and a solar-powered landing station on the ground for the real recharge between patrols. The 190 mm airframe shown below is shaped by a second constraint most solar-drone concepts ignore: on a quadrotor, the propellers occupy the exact real estate you'd want to put panels on.

Fig. 04.B — Design rationale

The geometry problem

The obvious layout is a flat solar deck across the top of the airframe. It is also wrong. That plate sits directly in the rotors' inflow and costs roughly 15–25% of available thrust — precisely the budget a solar project cannot afford to spend.

On an X-frame the four propeller discs sit on the diagonals, which leaves the ±X and ±Y axes clear. The panels therefore went onto four cruciform wings threading between the rotors, with 3.7 mm of measured clearance between wing edge and disc. Toggle Prop discs in the viewer above to see the constraint that drove the whole layout.

Because the model is fully parametric, the clearance is not a one-time hand check — it is a governing equation. Change the wheelbase or the propeller and the airframe regenerates and re-solves:

margin = (WB / 2)/√2 − Whalf − (Dprop/2 + clearance)

Four-inch propellers at this wheelbase return a negative margin — the model rejects the configuration before a single gram is printed.

Fig. 04.C — Energy budget

Letting the math change the goal

I ran the power budget before committing to hardware, because the answer determines whether the project is worth building at all.

Cell area118 cm²
Realistic harvest≈ 1.7 W
Pack energy (2S 850 mAh)6.3 Wh
Hover power draw≈ 70 W
Flight endurance≈ 4.3 min
Sunlight : flight≈ 60 : 1

Roughly one hour of sun per minute of flight. Sustained solar flight has only ever been achieved by fixed-wing aircraft with very large wing-area-to-mass ratios; a quadrotor's disc loading puts it out of reach by about 40× at this scale.

The number didn't kill the project — it relocated the solar. On the ground, panel area and buffer capacity are effectively free; on the airframe, every square centimetre is fought for in grams and prop clearance.

So the primary harvest moved to a solar-powered landing station: it charges a buffer battery all day, then fast-charges the aircraft on contact between patrols, turning the 60:1 ratio from a limit into a duty cycle. The onboard cells stay for what they are genuinely good at — topping up during loiter and extending reserve. The most valuable output of the energy model was a number that told me to redesign the system, not the aircraft.

Fig. 04.D — System & status

Build

The airframe is modelled parametrically in Python (CadQuery / OpenCASCADE) rather than drawn by hand, so wheelbase, propeller size, wing width and panel dimensions are driven variables. Six parts export to STEP for SolidWorks and STL for printing; all meshes are verified watertight and manifold before release.

Arms print in PETG-CF with layer lines running across the arm, not along it — layer adhesion is the governing failure mode. Solar side uses flexible ETFE monocrystalline cells into 1.2 mm recesses, feeding a CN3791 MPPT charge controller; a plain diode would discard roughly 30% of an already small harvest.

On the ground side, the solar landing station recharges the aircraft between patrol cycles, and the system is integrated into an active residential security setup with automated flight routing. Operations are geofenced to the property with camera coverage limited to the perimeter — on a surveillance platform, the privacy boundary is a design requirement, not an afterthought.

Status: In active development since May 2026. The airframe has been designed, 3D-printed and iterated for structural stiffness and payload capacity; the solar landing station is built and integrated into the security system's automated routing. The parametric revision shown above adds the four onboard solar wings, currently moving from CAD to print.
